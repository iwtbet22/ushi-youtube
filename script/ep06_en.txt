Our textbook is the Hiroshima Toyo Carp.
Every year at its shareholders' meeting, the Carp discloses its revenue, and part of the breakdown.

Merchandise sales were 2.53 billion yen.

The Carp drew 2.04 million fans in 2025.

But other clubs don't release numbers like these.
Companies must publish their results every year, but most clubs show only their profit, and a list of assets and liabilities.
So we can't tell how much revenue they had.
And the Giants and the Dragons don't publish their results at all.
There's one more rule to know when looking at a club's money.
In 1954, Japan's National Tax Agency issued a notice.
It said that money a parent company spends to cover its club's losses can be treated as advertising expenses, up to the amount of the loss.
Advertising is a business expense.
So when a parent company covers its club's losses, its own profit falls, and so does its tax bill.
Clubs have long been treated as advertising for their parent companies.
Because of this, a club's own numbers can change a lot depending on its ties to the parent.
If the parent pays a lot in advertising fees, the club shows a profit.
If the parent owns the stadium, stadium rent is just money moving inside the group.
So a club in the black isn't simply a club that's making money.
Clubs also fall into three groups by how they relate to their stadium.
The first: the group owns the stadium itself.
The second: the club rents its stadium from another company, or even a shrine.
The third: the club runs a stadium owned by a city or prefecture.
Which type a club is decides where the money from tickets, food, and ads ends up.
